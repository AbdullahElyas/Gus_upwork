Ankle Conclusion:

Ankle Assessment Conclusion: The Left foot: Your left foot was observed in a slightly everted position, with the centre of mass medial to the 2nd metatarsal. Despite the foot technically being able to pronate and supinate, you were unable to access pronation during assessment, indicating a lack of functional control. Both dorsiflexion and plantarflexion demonstrated poor range and strength, suggesting significant deficits in both mobility and power generation. There was limited articulation through the midfoot, and fascial control appeared underdeveloped. Building strength through overcoming isometrics and enhancing proprioceptive awareness should be prioritised.

The Right foot: The right foot was in a neutral position, with the centre of mass appropriately aligned over the 2nd metatarsal. Similar to the left, pronation could not be accessed, though the foot retains the mechanical ability to do so. Dorsiflexion range was poor, but strength was notably better than the left; plantarflexion, however, showed poor range and strength, mirroring the left side’s deficits.

Foot and Ankle summary: There is clear asymmetry between the feet, particularly in dorsiflexion and plantarflexion force and range, with the left foot being more compromised overall. Both feet lack functional pronation despite mechanical capability, and both demonstrate poor plantarflexion qualities. Key findings include insufficient midfoot articulation and underdeveloped fascial control, especially on the left. It is recommended to implement a structured isometric strengthening programme, focusing on both dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, alongside exercises to improve midfoot mobility and subconscious movement patterns. Incorporating dynamic balance and proprioceptive drills will help restore tissue length and improve overall foot and ankle function.
