Ankle Assessment Conclusion: Your left foot is in an everted position, with the centre of mass shifted medially, indicating a bias towards the 1st metatarsal. Although the foot is capable of both pronation and supination, you are unable to access pronation actively, suggesting a lack of dynamic control through the medial arch. Dorsiflexion range and strength are both good, but plantarflexion is notably limited in both range and strength. There is a clear need to prioritise plantarflexion strength, particularly through overcoming isometric exercises, and to develop greater proprioceptive awareness and midfoot control.

The right foot presents in a slightly everted position, with the centre of mass just medial to the 2nd metatarsal. Unlike the left, this foot can pronate but cannot access either pronation or supination actively, indicating a restriction in dynamic movement variability. Dorsiflexion is strong and unrestricted, while plantarflexion demonstrates good range but poor strength, highlighting a different profile to the left side.

Foot and Ankle summary: There is a degree of asymmetry between the two feet, particularly in plantarflexion range and the ability to access pronation and supination. Both feet show everted tendencies, but the left is more restricted in plantarflexion and lacks pronation control, while the right has better plantarflexion range but poor strength and limited dynamic movement options. Key findings include insufficient plantarflexion strength bilaterally, with the left foot also requiring improved range and neuromuscular control. We recommend a focused strengthening programme using overcoming isometrics for plantarflexion, alongside exercises to enhance midfoot articulation and fascial control. Incorporating subconscious movement drills, such as dynamic gait retraining and barefoot balance tasks, will help lengthen tissue and improve overall foot function.
