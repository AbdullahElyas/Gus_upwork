Ankle Assessment Conclusion: The Left foot: Your left foot is presenting in a slightly everted position, with the centre of mass medial to the 2nd metatarsal, indicating a tendency towards pronation. However, despite this alignment, the foot is unable to access pronation effectively, suggesting a restriction in midfoot articulation and fascial adaptability. Both dorsiflexion and plantarflexion demonstrate poor range and strength, highlighting significant deficits in both mobility and force production. There is a clear need to prioritise isometric strength development, particularly focusing on intrinsic foot muscles and ankle stabilisers, alongside improving proprioceptive awareness and control.

The Right foot: The right foot is in a neutral position, with the centre of mass aligned over the 2nd metatarsal. Like the left, it cannot pronate, but it does retain the capacity for both pronation and supination. Dorsiflexion shows poor range but maintains good strength, whereas plantarflexion is limited in both range and strength. Compared to the left, the right foot demonstrates relatively better dorsiflexion strength but shares similar deficits in plantarflexion.

Foot and Ankle summary: There is notable asymmetry between the feet, particularly in dorsiflexion and plantarflexion force and range, with the left foot more globally restricted. Both feet are unable to access pronation, indicating a need for improved midfoot articulation and fascial control. Key findings include bilateral plantarflexion weakness and restricted dorsiflexion, especially on the left. We recommend a structured isometric strengthening programme targeting both dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, with an emphasis on midfoot mobility drills and subconscious movement tasks (such as dynamic balance and gait retraining) to encourage tissue lengthening and neuromuscular adaptation. Focused proprioceptive exercises and controlled loading through the forefoot will be essential to restore symmetry and functional capacity.
